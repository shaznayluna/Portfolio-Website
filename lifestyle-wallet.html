Fan Wallet — Lifestyle Banking for a Football Club

Turning everyday banking into part of the matchday ritual. A mobile “lifestyle banking” app that blends a fully-featured fan wallet — payments, savings, loyalty, tickets and digital collectibles — into a single, club-branded experience. Designed as an investor-ready MVP prototype.

Role
Product / UX Designer (freelance)
Duration
~8 weeks (16 April – 13 June 2025)
Project Type
Investor MVP — end-to-end interactive prototype
Process
Straight from the client’s PRD to high-fidelity design and prototype — no wireframing stage

Overview

Most fans interact with their football club through a patchwork of apps: one for tickets, one for the shop, a loyalty card, a separate banking app, a betting app, a payment wallet. Each is a separate login, a separate balance, a separate moment of friction.

I was brought in to help design the opposite of that — a single app where the fan’s money and their fandom live in the same place. Salary lands, bills get sorted into “pots,” matchday tickets sit in a wallet, loyalty points buy real perks, and the trophies a fan earns by showing up are theirs to keep, show off, or sell.

The product sat at an unusual intersection: a regulated banking product with the emotional pull of a club membership and the playfulness of a game. My job was to make all three feel like one thing.

The problem

Qorbital had a compelling thesis — that brands with passionate audiences can deepen loyalty (and lifetime value) by owning the everyday banking relationship, not just the occasional transaction. But a thesis isn’t a product. The challenge was to prove, in a tappable prototype, that:

  • A fan would actually want their bank account to live inside a club app.
  • A genuinely deep feature set (accounts, payments, FX, savings, loyalty, ticketing, collectibles) could be made to feel simple, not overwhelming.
  • Banking moments could be reframed as fandom moments without feeling gimmicky or manipulative.

This was an MVP built to win investor and stakeholder confidence, so every screen had to read as both credible as a bank and delightful as a fan product.

Who I was designing for

The primary user was a committed, mobile-first fan — someone who already spends on tickets, kit, travel and matchday rounds, and who organises group activities (five-a-side, away trips, pub kitties) with friends. They’re motivated by belonging and status, comfortable with everyday fintech (Apple Pay, round-ups, splitting bills), and impatient with clunky processes.

I anchored the design around a single day in this fan’s life — waking up, organising a game with friends, heading to a match, and getting home — and used that narrative to pressure-test whether the features actually fit how a fan moves through their day, rather than designing screens in isolation.

My role & approach

As the product/UX designer I owned the experience end to end: information architecture, user flows, interaction design, the design system, and a high-fidelity, fully interactive Figma prototype.

This was fast-paced client work on a tight timeline — roughly eight weeks. To move at that speed we skipped the usual low-fidelity wireframing stage and went straight from the client’s PRD to high-fidelity design and prototype. That made building a design system early a necessity, not a luxury: a shared library of components, tokens and patterns was the only way to design a product this broad, keep it visually consistent, and still iterate fast enough to hit the deadline. The system became the foundation everything else was assembled from — every flow below was built from the same set of reusable parts.

The core design challenge

The hardest problem wasn’t any single screen — it was coherence. A banking app, a loyalty scheme, a ticketing platform and an NFT collection each come with their own conventions and mental models. Bolted together, they’d feel like four apps in a trench coat.

I treated the wallet as the spine of the whole product. Money, points, tickets and collectibles are all just things you hold — so I designed a consistent “asset” model and a unified statement (a “lifestyle statement”) where a season-ticket entry, a round-up into a savings pot, and loyalty points earned all read as part of one continuous story. That single decision is what let the breadth feel like depth instead of clutter.

Key flows I designed

The wallet & “lifestyle statement”

The home of the product. Balance, cards (digital and physical, controllable separately), and a transaction feed that goes beyond debits and credits — showing ticket spends, loyalty earned, round-ups, and itemised purchase detail where integrated. I designed it to feel like a bank statement that finally understands the user: every line connects to something they actually did as a fan.

Rewards & loyalty

Points earned through spending and engagement unlock tangible perks — early ticket access, exclusive kit drops, sponsor discounts applied automatically at point of sale. I designed loyalty as a clear, legible progression (“you’re 20 points from qualifying for the away game — here’s how to earn them”) rather than an opaque points balance, so the reward always felt within reach and the path to it was obvious.

Pots — shared and personal money goals

Round-up savings toward a goal (with an estimated “you’ll hit this by…” date), automatic bill pots on payday, and — the social heart of the feature — group pots for things like a five-a-side team kitty or an away-trip fund. Members can see contributions and spending in real time, send one-tap payment nudges to the friend who hasn’t paid, and watch unspent funds auto-refund proportionally when a pot closes. I spent a lot of time here on transparency and trust: shared money is socially fraught, so the design makes “who paid, who owes, where it went” unambiguous at a glance.

Matchday tickets & the Season-Ticket Exchange

Tickets live in the wallet as assets. The flow I’m proudest of is the exchange, which solves a real fan problem: a season-ticket holder who can’t make a game. From the ticket, a holder can:

  • Transfer directly to a named friend,
  • List it in an exchange pool (set a price, or give it free), or
  • Donate it to the club for a community or youth cause.

I mapped the full lifecycle, including the edge cases that make or break trust in a transfer system: ticket states moving from held → pending → transferred, anonymous vs. named listings, claim limits, and auto-revert if a listed ticket goes unclaimed close to kickoff. Designing the unhappy paths as carefully as the happy one is what made this feel like infrastructure rather than a demo.

The Trophy Room — fandom as a collection

The feature that most captured the emotional pitch. Fans earn digital collectibles — numbered matchday-shirt tokens, attendance awards (e.g. consecutive home games), memorabilia — which land in a personal trophy room. Each item is genuinely owned: a fan can reveal or hide their name on it, look up its edition number, and list it for sale or transfer to others, with the platform brokering the exchange safely.

I designed this to feel like a display cabinet, not a crypto wallet — the value on screen is status and memory, with the financial mechanics tucked quietly underneath. The privacy toggle (reveal/hide ownership) was a deliberate choice: collecting is social, but only on the fan’s terms.

Outcome

I delivered a reusable Figma design system and a complete, high-fidelity, interactive prototype spanning onboarding, the wallet, rewards, the ticket exchange and the trophy room — enough for stakeholders to walk the full “day in the life” of a fan and for Qorbital to use in investor conversations. Working straight from the PRD, the whole thing came together in roughly eight weeks. The work translated a broad, abstract “lifestyle banking” thesis into something concrete people could tap, react to, and believe in.

Most importantly, the prototype did its job as a fundraising asset. Qorbital took it into investor meetings and, shortly after delivery, reported strong momentum toward securing the investment needed to take the product forward — a clear signal that the design made the vision credible to the people who needed to back it.
